# Real-World Autonomous Driving Landscape: Generalization Research and Transferability to a Lightweight 2D Racing-RL Simulator

## How does mainstream real-world AD research define and measure generalization to unseen roads/scenarios? What benchmarks are used?

### Takeaway
Real-world AD research has moved from i.i.d. train/test splits (same city/distribution) toward explicit, engineered distribution-shift benchmarks (unseen towns/cities/countries, unseen agent behaviors, rare "long-tail" events) because standard benchmarks were found to let models succeed via memorization rather than genuine generalization; closed-loop, reactive-agent evaluation (nuPlan, NAVSIM v2) and long-tail-focused datasets (WOD-E2E) are now the state of the art for measuring this rigorously.

### Cited Findings
- nuPlan is a large-scale, closed-loop planning benchmark that simulates reactive urban traffic to evaluate ML-based, rule-based, and hybrid planners; "NuPlan's simulation and scoring protocols have driven a shift in research on generalization, robustness, and real-world deployment of planning systems," explicitly noting that "most existing benchmarks rely on i.i.d. evaluation... which often masks a critical lack of generalization as models may implicitly memorize map-specific features or localized traffic patterns." — [nuPlan-R paper / EmergentMind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/nuplan)
- NAVSIM is a data-driven, non-reactive benchmark built on OpenScene (a redistribution of nuPlan data); its core metric is the Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS), combining No Collision, Drivable Area Compliance, Ego Progress, Time-to-Collision, and Comfort submetrics. — [NAVSIM/nuPlan overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/nuplan-benchmark-52dc041d-e596-4342-b1b6-f4ffc15b905a)
- NAVSIM v2.0 (2025 iteration) extends PDMS to an "Extended PDMS" (EPDMS) adding Traffic Light Compliance, Driving Direction Compliance, Lane Keeping, and Extended Comfort; it introduces reactive background traffic and a two-stage "pseudo closed-loop" simulation using 3D Gaussian Splatting to synthesize counterfactual camera views after a policy deviates from the logged trajectory — explicitly framed as improving assessment of "robustness and generalization." Pseudo-simulation correlates strongly with full closed-loop simulation while using roughly 6x less compute. — [autonomousvision/navsim GitHub](https://github.com/autonomousvision/navsim)
- CARLA Leaderboard 2.0 trains agents on 4 towns with a fixed set of weather conditions and tests on 2 unseen towns (e.g., train on Town 12, validate on Town 13) plus unseen weather — an explicit train/unseen-environment generalization split; each leaderboard cycle can swap in new unseen-equivalent test sets specifically to keep testing generalization power rather than overfit performance. — [CARLA Leaderboard evaluation criteria](https://leaderboard.carla.org/evaluation_v2_1/)
- "Fail2Drive" (2026) argues existing closed-loop CARLA benchmarks "reuse training scenarios at test time," letting systems succeed through memorization; it introduces a paired-route benchmark (200 CARLA routes, each with an in-distribution and a shifted twin) across four shift categories — appearance, layout, behavioral, and robustness — and reports current SOTA models show an average 22.8% success-rate drop under distribution shift, plus qualitative failures such as models "ignoring objects clearly visible in the LiDAR and failing to learn the fundamental concepts of free and occupied space." — [Fail2Drive (arXiv 2604.08535)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.08535)
- "Shift & Drift" (2026) is a zero-shot generalization benchmark built by converting the DeepScenario Open 3D dataset into the nuPlan simulation framework, creating 1,182 test scenarios; it tests two shift axes — semantic shift (planners trained on North American/Singaporean data evaluated on German cities and San Francisco with dense pedestrian-cyclist interactions) and state-distribution drift (injected stochastic actuation noise). Finding: imitation-learning planners perform well in-distribution but fail significantly under pedestrian-dense semantic shift and suffer persistent drift under actuation noise; a tested RL-based planner degraded more gracefully. — [Shift & Drift (arXiv 2607.07844)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07844)
- "Beyond Self-Play and Scale" (BehaviorBench, 2026) argues that large-scale self-play RL policies (citing GigaFlow, PufferDrive) "remain disconnected from established benchmarks," and that self-play-trained policies "overfit to their training opponents and fail to generalize to other traffic agent behaviors." It connects PufferDrive-trained RL policies to nuPlan for standardized evaluation and pulls interaction-rich scenarios from the Waymo Open Motion Dataset specifically because prior benchmarks were too easy ("near-perfect scores achievable by straight lane following"). — [BehaviorBench (arXiv 2605.10034)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10034)
- Waymo's WOD-E2E dataset (2025-2026) targets vision-based end-to-end driving on rare, safety-critical long-tail scenarios: 4,021 segments (~12 hours) selected from a multi-million-mile corpus, focused on events occurring below 0.03% frequency in daily driving. A case study on 6,391,012 miles of logs found only 6,888 miles (0.1%) met long-tail criteria, and after manual review (30% conversion rate) the true long-tail fraction was ~0.03%. Waymo proposes a "Rater Feedback Score" (RFS) — comparing predicted trajectories to human-rater trajectory preference labels — instead of pure log-distance metrics, precisely because measuring generalization to rare events needs different evaluation than ordinary driving. — [WOD-E2E (arXiv 2510.26125)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26125)
- A 2026 paper on cross-domain driving world models found that combining geographically diverse training data (Pittsburgh, Boston, Singapore) reduced a "mean surprise score" (a generalization proxy) by 16.5% relative to single-geography (nuPlan-only) training (0.228 vs. 0.273), and that tripling the volume of single-geography data (200,000 Argoverse2-only scenarios) still produced worse generalization (0.264 surprise) than a combined 63K-scenario multi-geography model — i.e., geographic diversity beat data volume as a predictor of cross-domain generalization. — [Geographic Diversity paper (arXiv 2607.04500)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04500)

### Inferences
- The field's working definition of "generalization" has concretely operationalized into: (a) held-out geography/town splits, (b) held-out weather, (c) held-out or reactive/adversarial traffic-agent behavior, (d) rare/long-tail event coverage, and (e) robustness to actuation/sensing noise — not just lower loss on a held-out i.i.d. test set.
- There is a visible trend (2025-2026) of the field explicitly critiquing its own older benchmarks as too easy/memorizable and building second-generation benchmarks (Fail2Drive, Shift & Drift, BehaviorBench, NAVSIM v2, WOD-E2E) specifically to close that gap — this is very recent and still an active, unsettled area, not a solved methodology.

### Gaps
- I could not find a single canonical definition of "generalization" agreed upon across the field; different papers use different shift taxonomies (appearance/layout/behavioral/robustness vs. semantic/state-distribution) with no clear consolidation yet.
- Could not verify precise dataset/compute sizes for nuPlan/nuScenes training runs beyond a couple of example configurations (see resource-question section below) — these are anecdotal from individual papers, not documented as field-wide norms.

## What recent (2023-2026) end-to-end/foundation-model approaches to driving exist, and what do they report about cross-geography/road-type generalization?

### Takeaway
The leading real-world example of explicit cross-geography generalization claims is Wayve's foundation-model "AI Driver" program, which reports concrete before/after numbers for adapting a UK-trained model to the US and Germany; academic literature (2024-2026) is separately producing driving world models (Vista, MUVO, Metis, etc.) and vision-language-action (VLA) models for driving, but rigorous, quantified cross-geography generalization claims outside Wayve's own publications are comparatively rare and often self-reported by the company rather than independently verified.

### Cited Findings
- Wayve is training a single foundation-model "AI Driver" with the explicit goal of driving in 500 cities by end of 2025 "with no retraining and no region-specific coding"; as of the roadshow report it had completed legs covering 90 cities in 90 days across Europe, North America, and Asia, logging over 10,000 supervised-driving hours (half in dense urban areas), including in Montana highways, rural Italy, and Yokohama backstreets, without HD maps or region-specific logic. — [Wayve AI-500 Roadshow](https://wayve.ai/thinking/ai-500-roadshow-90-cities/)
- Wayve reports quantified adaptation results moving a UK-trained model to the US (a right-hand-drive to left-hand-drive shift): zero-shot US performance initially lagged UK levels; after 100 hours of US-specific data, performance improved "fivefold"; after 500 hours (collected over 8 weeks), it reached a "40x improvement," reaching UK-equivalent performance in urban and highway environments. Zero-shot (no fine-tuning) performance in Germany was reported as "3x better" than the initial US zero-shot deployment, attributed to accumulated diverse market exposure. Training on a mix of UK+US data produced "consistently lower loss curves... including previously unseen environments like Germany," and adding 10,000 hours of combined UK/US/Germany data produced a further "3x performance increase" versus UK-only data. A separate vehicle-platform swap saw an "8x" improvement from 100 hours of vehicle-specific data. — [Wayve "Crossing the Pond and Beyond"](https://wayve.ai/thinking/multi-country-generalization/)
- Wayve uses synthetic-data/simulation tools (named GAIA, PRISM, Ghost Gym) to generate "high-quality, photorealistic simulations" specifically to test safety-critical edge cases that "would take years to encounter in the real world" — i.e., simulation is explicitly used to supplement, not replace, real-world safety validation. No total compute or full dataset-size figures were disclosed in this source. — [Wayve "Crossing the Pond and Beyond"](https://wayve.ai/thinking/multi-country-generalization/)
- Wayve's LINGO-2 is described as "the first vision-language-action model tested on public roads," generating driving behavior and continuous natural-language commentary from the same model, and can be steered by constrained navigation-style language commands. — [LINGO-2](https://wayve.ai/press/introducing-lingo-2/)
- OpenDriveLab's Vista (2024) is presented as a "generalizable driving world model" for high-fidelity, action-conditioned, long-horizon future prediction "in the wild," also usable as a generalizable reward function to assess driving behavior quality. — [DriveAGI / OpenDriveLab collection](https://github.com/OpenDriveLab/DriveAGI)
- Industry commentary (2025) projects that "VLA-centric end-to-end solutions" will exceed 50% of L3/L4 AD markets by 2030, and notes companies like Li Auto integrating end-to-end + VLM approaches into VLA architectures in 2025 — this is market/industry-report framing, not a peer-reviewed generalization claim, and should be read as forward-looking industry positioning rather than confirmed fact. — [End-to-End AD Research Report 2025 (ResearchInChina)](https://www.researchinchina.com/Htmls/Report/2025/77076.html)
- comma.ai's openpilot uses a system-level end-to-end neural network (camera-to-trajectory) trained on real-world driving data uploaded by its user fleet; the model is not explicitly taught lane lines/intersections/traffic rules but infers behavior from human driving examples across "millions of miles" of fleet data. Its 0.11 release (2024/2025 era) is described as the start of a paradigm relying solely on neural networks from planning through simulating driving video. — [comma.ai blog, 0.11 release](https://blog.comma.ai/011release/)
- UniAD ("Planning-oriented Autonomous Driving," CVPR 2023) is a unified end-to-end Transformer-based stack jointly learning perception, prediction, and planning from dense BEV representations; downstream work reports that when trained purely on generated (synthetic) video clips rather than real data, UniAD's collision rate roughly doubles (reported 0.34 → 0.50), indicating synthetic data cannot yet fully substitute for real driving data in this architecture. — [UniAD/synthetic-data follow-up work, via search summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/unified-autonomous-driving-uniad); original: [UniAD paper](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2023/papers/Hu_Planning-Oriented_Autonomous_Driving_CVPR_2023_paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strongest, most concrete cross-geography generalization evidence in this space comes from a single company's own self-published reports (Wayve) rather than from independently peer-reviewed, third-party-verified benchmarks — the underlying methodology and raw data for these specific numbers (e.g., "40x improvement") are not independently auditable from what I found, so they should be treated as credible-but-unverified industry claims, not settled scientific fact.
- The academic direction (world models, VLA models, unified end-to-end stacks) is converging with industry (Wayve, Li Auto) around "foundation model trained on very large heterogeneous driving corpora, then lightly fine-tuned/adapted per-geography" as the dominant 2024-2026 generalization strategy, rather than hand-engineered geography-specific rules.

### Gaps
- I could not independently verify Wayve's specific quantitative claims (e.g., "40x," "3x," "8x" improvements) against a peer-reviewed paper; these come from Wayve's own blog/press content, which is a primary but self-interested source.
- I did not find a rigorous academic paper directly quantifying Tesla's or other major players' (e.g., Waabi's) cross-geography generalization performance with comparable specificity — Tesla in particular publishes little formal research on this exact question, and I found no citable data from Tesla for this notes file.

## What are the concrete structural differences between closed-track racing-RL and open-road real-world AD that would make techniques not transfer cleanly?

### Takeaway
The literature consistently frames racing as a valuable but narrow testbed for control/perception under extreme dynamics, explicitly distinct from the safety-critical, interaction-heavy, long-tail-dominated, open-network problem that defines real-world AD generalization research; several benchmark papers found above (BehaviorBench, Shift & Drift) directly show that policies which do well in constrained/self-play settings fail specifically on the dimensions that don't exist in a closed-track, no-traffic racing setup (dense pedestrian interaction, rare-event coverage, reactive multi-agent behavior).

### Cited Findings
- The F1TENTH/RoboRacer platform survey (2025) frames racing environments as "ideal for testing control systems and perception algorithms due to the time-critical nature of these tasks, the necessity for rapid decision-making, and the inherent complexity of the environment" — i.e., the case for racing-as-testbed is about control/perception under speed and adversarial dynamics, not about traffic-rule compliance, pedestrian interaction, or open road-network navigation. — [RoboRacer/F1TENTH survey (arXiv 2506.15899)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15899)
- The same body of racing research explicitly frames racing as complementary to, not a substitute for, ordinary AD research: "routine driving situations dominate autonomous vehicle operations, [but] focusing on extreme situations and environments is crucial to support investigation into safety benefits" — i.e., racing is pitched as edge-case/limit-handling research, deliberately outside the routine-driving distribution that dominates real deployment. — [search synthesis citing F1TENTH/racing survey literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15899)
- A search specifically for "racing as poor proxy" framing found no paper explicitly arguing racing is a bad proxy for real-world AD generalization, but it did surface direct statements about racing's own generalization limitations: "poor generalization capabilities of most data-driven perception algorithms may lead to unsatisfactory behavior in many edge cases often encountered in racing, like the presence of debris on the track, or a vehicle emitting smoke or spinning out of control," and "since each racing track has its peculiar challenges, methods often require a different set of heuristics and parameters for each new situation, which effectively limits their generalization and scalability," and "end-to-end dynamics models generalize poorly to data outside of the training distribution... existing solutions are characterized by poor generalization to unseen road conditions." — [Autonomous Vehicles on the Edge: A Survey on AV Racing (arXiv 2202.07008)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.07008)
- BehaviorBench's finding that self-play-trained RL driving policies "overfit to their training opponents and fail to generalize to other traffic agent behaviors" is a direct empirical demonstration of a structural gap: a closed, self-consistent training distribution (analogous to a closed racing track with fixed/no traffic) does not transfer to open, heterogeneous real-world agent behavior. — [BehaviorBench (arXiv 2605.10034)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10034)
- Shift & Drift's finding that imitation-learning planners "exhibit significant failures under semantic shift, particularly in pedestrian-dense environments" is direct evidence that a training/deployment domain lacking dense pedestrian/cyclist interaction (as in a closed-track racing sim with no pedestrians) is a fundamentally different — and, per this paper, harder — problem than the one racing addresses. — [Shift & Drift (arXiv 2607.07844)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07844)
- WOD-E2E's framing (rare events occurring at <0.03% frequency, requiring mining millions of miles to find a few thousand usable long-tail miles) illustrates a structural resourcing gap: real-world AD generalization research is fundamentally about statistically rare, safety-critical long-tail events that a closed-loop, single-track, no-traffic racing simulator has no mechanism to generate or represent at all (there is no equivalent "long tail" of pedestrian/cross-traffic/occlusion events in a track with no other agents). — [WOD-E2E (arXiv 2510.26125)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26125)

### Inferences
- The core structural differences that would block clean transfer are: (1) presence/absence of dynamic, heterogeneous, adversarial-or-cooperative other agents (pedestrians, cyclists, other vehicles with unpredictable intent) — racing RL as scoped in the current project has none; (2) open road network topology and traffic-rule compliance (intersections, right-of-way, signage) vs. a closed, fixed track with only a lap-time/progress reward; (3) the long-tail safety distribution that dominates real AD risk (rare pedestrian/occlusion/adverse-weather events) vs. racing's "edge cases" being about vehicle dynamics limits (skids, debris, spin-outs), which are a different kind of edge case entirely; (4) reward structure — lap time/progress is a dense, well-shaped, single-objective signal, whereas real AD requires balancing progress against collision avoidance, comfort, legality, and interaction courtesy simultaneously, which is a qualitatively different multi-objective and much sparser-safety-reward problem.
- Because current real-world AD generalization benchmarks are explicitly being redesigned (2025-2026) around agent-interaction density and long-tail rarity — precisely the two axes a closed-track, no-traffic racing sim lacks by construction — techniques validated purely on racing-RL benchmarks would very likely need substantial re-architecture (new reward design, new agent-interaction modeling, entirely new evaluation scenarios) rather than simple fine-tuning to be relevant to real AD generalization research.

### Gaps
- I did not find a single paper that directly and explicitly argues "racing RL is/isn't a good proxy for real-world AD generalization" as its central thesis — this exact comparative argument does not appear to exist as a dedicated publication; the inference above is constructed by triangulating racing-domain limitation statements against AD-domain structural requirements, not from one source making this exact comparison.

## Is there existing research that explicitly bridges racing/simulated RL and real-world driving?

### Takeaway
The clearest and best-documented bridge is Sony AI/Polyphony's Gran Turismo Sophy, but this is explicitly a racing-simulator achievement whose authors themselves note has not been demonstrated to transfer to real-world physical driving; F1TENTH is positioned as a racing sim-to-real testbed but for scaled RC-car racing (not general urban driving), and no paper was found that uses a racing environment as a stepping-stone later validated specifically on real-world urban/general driving data.

### Cited Findings
- Gran Turismo Sophy (Sony AI, Polyphony Digital, Sony Interactive Entertainment) used deep RL to beat champion human drivers in the Gran Turismo Sport game, published in Nature (2022); it was trained using inputs like speed, wheel angle, and track curvature to learn "race car control, racing tactics, and racing etiquette," and the GT Sport simulator itself is noted to incorporate real vehicle-dynamics modeling (air resistance, tire friction, suspension) drawn from real racing technical support work. — [Nature paper via search summary](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04357-7); [Sony/GT Sophy technology page](https://www.gran-turismo.com/us/gran-turismo-sophy/technology/)
- Despite this, the search summary explicitly notes: "despite its success in simulation, this method has not yet demonstrated effective real-world transferability to actual physical racing environments" — this is a search-tool synthesis statement, not a direct quote from the Nature paper itself, so I flag it as likely accurate but not independently verified against primary text.
- F1TENTH/RoboRacer explicitly targets the sim-to-real gap as a core research theme ("Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap under Real-Time Constraints in Autonomous Racing," 2026) using a ROS+Gazebo simulator designed to mirror a real 1/10-scale RC racecar platform, allowing algorithm verification in sim before real-car deployment — but this bridges a *racing simulator* to a *real racing car*, not to general real-world (open-road, multi-agent, traffic-law) driving. — [F1TENTH sim-to-real (arXiv 2607.18586)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18586)

### Inferences
- No paper was found that explicitly uses a racing environment (F1TENTH, CARLA racing scenarios, Gran Turismo) as a deliberate stepping-stone toward techniques later validated on real-world general/urban driving data or benchmarks (nuPlan, nuScenes, WOMD). The bridging work that does exist stays within racing (racing-sim to real racecar), not racing-to-general-AD.
- This absence is itself informative: it suggests the research community does not currently treat racing RL as a recognized methodological pathway into real-world AD generalization research — the two communities (racing RL / F1TENTH-Sophy-style, and real-world AD / nuPlan-Waymo-Wayve-style) appear to operate largely in parallel with limited cross-citation on the generalization question specifically.

## What does real-world AD research say about simulation fidelity, sensor realism, and domain gap — and what would a lightweight 2D team need to add?

### Takeaway
Recent research (2024-2026) shows the sim-to-real domain gap for real sensors (especially LiDAR) is driven by subtle physical effects — not just geometry — and that even small unmodeled effects (e.g., ~5% of points from secondary LiDAR returns) can materially change downstream safety-critical planning decisions; this implies that a 2D simulator with simplified GPS/IMU/lidar/pose sensors is many fidelity tiers below what current AD research treats as meaningful sensor realism, and closing that gap would require essentially building (or licensing) a 3D, sensor-realistic simulator — a substantial undertaking distinct from the current project's scope.

### Cited Findings
- Waabi's LiDAR domain-gap research identifies six specific real-LiDAR phenomena that naive/classic simulators miss: unreturned pulses (causing overly dense simulated point clouds), multiple echoes/secondary reflections (especially at object borders and vegetation), spurious returns (multi-path effects, blooming off reflective objects, beam divergence, volume scattering), noisy points (ambiguous waveform peaks affecting thin structures/retroreflectors), non-uniform real scanning patterns (vs. the equidistant beams common in classic simulators), and motion artifacts from rolling-shutter-style scanning during a ~100ms sweep. — [Waabi LiDAR domain-gap research](https://waabi.ai/research/lidar-dg)
- Waabi's methodology pairs digital twins of real scenes against real sensor captures of the identical scenario, checking whether downstream perception/prediction/planning outputs match — and finds that material/reflectance modeling and accurate actor-motion-during-sweep modeling drive the largest realism gains, and that autonomy systems are not invariant to point-density variation across the scene. Critically, they report that subtle effects affecting only ~5% of points (secondary LiDAR returns) can substantially change downstream safety-critical detection/prediction/planning decisions. — [Waabi LiDAR domain-gap research](https://waabi.ai/research/lidar-dg)
- Broader domain-gap literature (S2R-Bench, 2025) states sensor simulation fidelity is uneven across modalities: "camera models are mature, but radar and LiDAR simulations remain less accurate under adverse conditions," and that simulated data is typically "more idealized and consistent" than real data, which includes sensor noise and simplified material/texture/reflectivity properties. — [S2R-Bench (Nature Scientific Data)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-025-06255-3)
- NeRF/Gaussian-Splatting-based (data-driven, reconstructed-from-real-footage) simulators are reported to exhibit a smaller domain gap than traditional artist-built/procedural simulators, because they are optimized directly against real sensor observations rather than hand-authored geometry/materials. — [Domain gap survey summary, "From Virtual Environments to Real-World Trials" (arXiv 2603.17714)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17714)
- UniAD trained purely on generated/synthetic video clips (rather than real driving footage) saw its collision rate roughly double (0.34 → 0.50 in the reported figure), a direct demonstration that current synthetic-data generation — even from a sophisticated generative pipeline — is not yet a full substitute for real sensor/driving data for safety-relevant metrics. — [via search synthesis, UniAD-related work](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/unified-autonomous-driving-uniad)

### Inferences
- The current project's simplified GPS/IMU/lidar/pose sensor stack (as described in the task's framing) is far below the fidelity tier that real-world AD domain-gap research treats as necessary — the research finds that even sophisticated, purpose-built LiDAR simulators with digital-twin-level scene reconstruction still have measurable, safety-relevant domain gaps; a 2D lidar/pose abstraction (no ray-material interaction, no scanning-pattern realism, no multi-echo/noise modeling) would not be expected to produce results transferable to real sensor-based AD without a fundamental sensor-simulation rebuild.
- To make results "relevant to real-world driving at all" in the sense this research community would recognize, a team would minimally need: (a) a 3D environment with real-scale road/agent geometry (2D racing sims abstract this away entirely), (b) sensor models that at least approximate the specific noise/multi-echo/material effects identified above for any LiDAR-based claims, (c) dynamic multi-agent traffic with heterogeneous, non-scripted behavior, and (d) an evaluation protocol built around held-out geography/scenario splits and long-tail/safety metrics rather than lap time — i.e., essentially converging toward a CARLA-like or NAVSIM-like setup rather than extending a 2D racing sim.

### Gaps
- I did not find quantitative figures for exactly how much simulation/sensor fidelity is "enough" to produce transferable real-world results (e.g., a defined fidelity threshold) — the literature describes what causes domain gap and that closing it matters, but I found no source giving a specific fidelity bar below which results are considered non-transferable versus above which they are considered valid.

## What are the resource/data requirements of real-world AD research relative to a lightweight, open-source, small-team racing-RL project — is scope expansion realistic?

### Takeaway
Real-world AD generalization research is resourced at a scale (multi-million-mile fleets, petabyte-scale datasets, 500+ city deployment programs, dedicated safety-validation teams, and even "small" industry players like comma.ai relying on a large user fleet for real-world data) that is structurally incompatible with a single/small open-source team's typical resourcing; meaningful, literature-recognized scope expansion into "general autonomous driving generalization" would likely require access to one of the major public datasets/benchmarks (nuPlan, nuScenes, WOMD, CARLA) rather than attempting to generate comparable real-world-relevant data or infrastructure independently.

### Cited Findings
- nuScenes provides ~1.4M camera images, ~390k LiDAR sweeps, ~1.4M radar sweeps, and ~1.4M object bounding boxes across 40k keyframes from 1,000 urban driving scenes (~20 seconds each, ~5 hours total driving). — [search synthesis of nuScenes dataset stats](https://motional.com/news/motionals-nuplan-dataset-set-usher-next-generation-autonomous-vehicles)
- nuPlan delivers roughly 500 million images and 100 million LiDAR scans, totaling 1,500 hours of driving (~4.7 years of average driving). — [search synthesis of nuPlan dataset stats](https://github.com/motional/nuplan-devkit)
- Example training-compute footprints found for models built on these datasets: one NAVSIM-based study trained on 8x NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs (30 epochs, ~5 hours); another nuScenes-benchmark study trained with batch size 64 across 64x NVIDIA H20 GPUs — illustrating that even using pre-collected public datasets (i.e., without needing to run a real vehicle fleet), current SOTA AD model training still commonly requires multi-GPU clusters, not single-GPU/consumer hardware. — [search synthesis, various 2025-2026 papers]
- Waymo's long-tail mining example required searching 6,391,012 miles of real fleet driving logs to find 6,888 miles (0.1%) of long-tail-candidate data, refined via manual review to a final ~0.03% long-tail fraction (~4,021 curated segments, ~12 hours) — illustrating that generating a research-grade long-tail/generalization dataset requires an existing multi-million-mile real fleet, which is well beyond small-team reach. — [WOD-E2E (arXiv 2510.26125)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26125)
- Wayve's generalization program is described in terms of 90 cities in 90 days, 10,000+ supervised driving hours, and later a stated goal of 500 cities — again requiring an operating real-vehicle fleet with safety drivers across multiple countries. — [Wayve AI-500 Roadshow](https://wayve.ai/thinking/ai-500-roadshow-90-cities/)
- By contrast, comma.ai is presented as a relatively lean company (compared to Waymo/Cruise-scale AV programs) that nonetheless depends on a large external user/customer fleet uploading real driving data ("millions of miles") to train its end-to-end model — i.e., even the leanest well-known real-world end-to-end AD effort still depends on a real-vehicle data pipeline that a purely simulation-based small team does not have access to. — [comma.ai blog / openpilot description](https://blog.comma.ai/011release/)
- Public benchmarks (nuPlan, nuScenes, WOMD, CARLA, NAVSIM) are open and freely usable by outside researchers without needing a fleet, and CARLA specifically is a synthetic/simulated environment (not requiring real sensor data at all) — this is the one clear resourcing path by which a small team could engage with real-AD-recognized generalization research without owning a vehicle fleet. — [CARLA leaderboard](https://leaderboard.carla.org/)

### Inferences
- Direct replication of the real-world AD research approach (collecting real fleet data, running physical safety validation, deploying across cities) is unambiguously not realistic for a small/single-team open-source racing-RL project — this would require fleet operations, safety drivers, regulatory engagement, and sensor hardware entirely outside typical open-source project resourcing.
- A more realistic (but still substantial) path to engaging with this research area would be adopting an existing open simulator with multi-agent traffic and open road networks (most plausibly CARLA, given it's free, simulation-only, and has an established generalization-benchmark structure) rather than building/extending sensor-realistic simulation from scratch or attempting to use real-world datasets like nuPlan/nuScenes (which assume real sensor data and closed-loop replay infrastructure that a 2D racing sim does not have).
- Even the CARLA path represents a substantial scope and engineering jump from the current project (2D, closed-track, no-traffic racing) — it would mean essentially starting a new, largely unrelated project (3D simulation, multi-agent traffic AI, road network/traffic-rule modeling, new evaluation harness) rather than an incremental expansion of the existing racing-RL codebase.

### Gaps
