# Scope Decision: Competitive/Community Landscape for Racing-RL Generalization

## What active competitions/benchmarks exist for racing RL, what do they measure, do any explicitly reward cross-track generalization, and what is their 2023–2026 activity level?

### Takeaway
There is a real, active cluster of racing-RL competitions (F1TENTH/RoboRacer, Indy Autonomous Challenge), but most of them measure head-to-head speed/overtaking on a *known* map rather than cross-track generalization; the one competition that explicitly targeted cross-track/cross-domain generalization as its scoring criterion (the Learn-to-Race Challenge) appears to have run once (2022) and shows no evidence of continuing, while two other historically important racing-RL efforts (Roborace, AWS DeepRacer League) have been formally discontinued. Gran Turismo Sophy is a research artifact, not a competition, and it is still active as of 2025–2026 with new generalization-focused publications.

### Cited Findings
- **F1TENTH / RoboRacer** (rebranded from "F1TENTH" to "RoboRacer") is a recurring, currently-active competition series: the "24th Roboracer Autonomous Grand Prix" ran at ICRA 2025 with both an in-person hardware race and a virtual simulation race, IV 2025 hosted a RoboRacer call for teams in Cluj-Napoca in June 2025, the "25th Roboracer Competition" took place in June 2026, and a further F1TENTH Autonomous Racing Challenge ran at the University of Victoria in April 2026, plus a planned IROS 2026 race — [IEEE ICRA 2025 event page](https://2025.ieee-icra.org/event/f1tenth-autonomous-racing-competition-proposal-for-icra-2025/); [IEEE IV 2025 call for teams](https://ieee-iv.org/2025/call-for-roboracer-teams/).
- F1TENTH's own virtual-race rules (ICRA 2022–2024 seasons) score a **Time Trial** (lap time, no collisions) and a **2-vehicle Head-to-Head** race, and separately test obstacle avoidance where "obstacles ... are unknown prior to run time" — i.e., the *obstacle layout* is held out, but the underlying **map is the same for Time Trial and Head-to-Head**, so the competition does not appear to score zero-shot performance across a held-out *track geometry* the way this project's problem statement does — [F1TENTH ICRA 2023 Rules](https://icra2023-race.f1tenth.org/rules.html).
- **Indy Autonomous Challenge (IAC)** is active and growing: it achieved what it calls the "world's first" completed multi-car autonomous race at CES 2025 (four AV-24 cars, no collisions, decided by <0.3s), and its own 2025→2026 recap states 9,288 autonomous miles across 7 circuits (ovals, road courses, runways, international venues) with 10 university teams in the 2025 season — [Indy Autonomous Challenge CES 2025 results](https://www.indyautonomouschallenge.com/ces-2025-results); [IAC 2025→2026 recap (X/Twitter)](https://x.com/IndyAChallenge/status/2007112501700292655). IAC races full-scale vehicles on real racetracks and is oriented toward perception/planning/control at racing speed and multi-car interaction, not explicitly toward a cross-track RL generalization benchmark.
- **Roborace** was discontinued: Arrival (the lead sponsor) ended the Roborace programme in May 2022 after failing to find alternative funding, and Arrival itself later entered administration and sold its assets to Canoo — [Roborace Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roborace).
- **AWS DeepRacer League** — a very high-participation but track-following/time-trial-oriented RL competition — held its final global championship at re:Invent 2024 and is being sunset as an AWS-hosted service; as of December 15, 2025 DeepRacer is no longer available in the AWS Console or for device purchase, though AWS released the underlying code as a self-hostable "DeepRacer on AWS" open-source solution in early 2025/January 2026 — [AWS: "The end of an era" blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/the-end-of-an-era-the-final-aws-deepracer-league-championship-at-reinvent-2024/); [AWS AI/ML Community: "DeepRacer is back!" (2026)](https://blog.awsaicommunity.org/posts/deepracer-is-back-2026/).
- **Learn-to-Race (L2R) Challenge 2022** is the one competition found that explicitly framed and scored **cross-domain/cross-track generalization** as a core evaluation axis: Stage 1 evaluated speed+safety on a known track (Thruxton), Stage 2 required the agent to "adapt to an unseen racetrack (Las Vegas) through safe exploration." It drew substantial participation for a research challenge (>20,100 views, 437 active participants, 46 teams, 733 submissions, 88 institutions) and was co-run with CMU, Arrival, AICrowd, AWS, and Honda Research — [Learn-to-Race Challenge 2022 paper (arXiv:2205.02953)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.02953); [AIcrowd L2R challenge page](https://www.aicrowd.com/challenges/learn-to-race-autonomous-racing-virtual-challenge).
- In the L2R 2022 results, teams hit 100% success and up to 152.09 km/h on the known Thruxton track, but on the unseen Las Vegas track the best generalizing approach only reached a 1.0 success rate at a reduced speed of 92.53 km/h — a concrete, citable demonstration of a real cross-track generalization gap, and a notable finding was that **modular perception+MPC pipelines beat end-to-end RL** on this generalization task — [Learn-to-Race Challenge 2022 paper (arXiv:2205.02953)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.02953).
- The L2R GitHub repo's most recent visible activity in search results is from December 2023, and no evidence was found of an L2R Challenge after 2022 (the SL4AD@ICML2022 and AI4AD@IJCAI2022 tracks were the only official challenge instances found) — [learn-to-race/l2r GitHub](https://github.com/learn-to-race/l2r). This is not a confirmed "archived" status, just an absence of newer activity in what was searchable.
- **Gran Turismo Sophy (Sony AI)** is not a competition but a research/product program; it is still actively developed as of 2025–2026: GT Sophy 2.0 (Nov 2023) expanded from 4 to 340+ cars across 9 tracks, new tracks (Nürburgring 24h, Autodrome Lago Maggiore) were added through 2024–2025, Sony AI published a "Five Years On" retrospective in 2026, and a new AAAI 2026 paper (SPARC, arXiv:2511.09737) reports state-of-the-art out-of-distribution generalization across 100+ unseen vehicle dynamics in GT7 — but this line of work generalizes across **vehicle dynamics**, not primarily across unseen **track geometry** — [Sony AI: "GT Sophy, Five Years On"](https://ai.sony/blog/gran-turismo-sophy-five-years-on-from-nature-cover-to-open-frontier); [SPARC paper, arXiv:2511.09737](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09737).
- A follow-on GT Sophy paper, "A Champion-level Vision-based RL Agent for Competitive Racing in Gran Turismo 7" (arXiv:2504.09021), notes that GT Sophy's inference "relies on global features, such as detailed track layout information and opponent localization, which are easily accessible in simulators but ... challenging to obtain in real-world scenarios," and frames generalizing/adapting to new situations "like humans do" (vs. retraining from scratch) as "a remaining frontier" — an explicit acknowledgment from the Sony AI line of work that unseen-track/unseen-situation generalization is still open, even in their flagship system — [arXiv:2504.09021](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09021).
- The GT Sophy Nature paper itself (Wurman et al., *Nature* 602:223–228, 2022) is about superhuman racecraft/tactics (overtaking, blocking, fair racing etiquette) on known Gran Turismo tracks with champion drivers, not cross-track zero-shot generalization — [Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04357-7).

### Inferences
- The competitive landscape for racing RL is bifurcated: (1) hardware/systems-heavy competitions (F1TENTH/RoboRacer, IAC) that are alive and growing but score head-to-head performance on known/shared maps, not held-out track geometry, and (2) a single research-challenge instance (L2R 2022) that directly matches this project's "unseen track geometry" framing but has no confirmed continuation since 2022.
- This suggests the specific narrow niche — a lightweight, reproducible, explicitly cross-track-generalization RL benchmark — is not currently being actively contested by a live, ongoing competition. The closest analog (L2R) validates that the problem is real and citable (it produced a concrete generalization-gap number: ~39% speed drop, 92.53 vs 152.09 km/h) but the competition infrastructure itself looks dormant, which is an opportunity rather than a crowding risk.
- Discontinuation of Roborace and AWS DeepRacer, both of which had significant community/participation scale, indicates that racing-RL competitions have historically struggled with sustained institutional funding/hosting, independent of research merit — worth flagging as a risk if this project's scope decision depends on assuming an active competition ecosystem will exist to benchmark against or attract collaborators.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm definitively whether L2R Challenge ran again after 2022, or whether AIcrowd or the l2r GitHub org has any 2024–2026 activity beyond what surfaced in search (repo's last visible update was reported as December 2023; this is search-result evidence, not a verified git log).
- Could not find a citable, recent (2024–2026) statement from F1TENTH/RoboRacer organizers about whether they plan to add a held-out-track generalization criterion to future seasons.
- Did not find independent confirmation of Roborace's precise "discontinued" status beyond the 2022 funding-loss reporting (i.e., no evidence found of any relaunch attempt since).

## Is there a citable open-source niche for a "lightweight, cheap-to-run, reproducible cross-track generalization benchmark," or is this already crowded/solved?

### Takeaway
The niche is real and not obviously solved: existing lightweight open racing environments (F1TENTH gym, MicroRacer, CarRacing) provide single/few-track RL tasks with baselines, and the one benchmark built specifically around cross-track generalization (L2R) is heavier-weight (uses the Arrival/Unreal-based high-fidelity simulator) and appears dormant as an active challenge. A lightweight, 2D, explicitly-cross-track-generalization-scored benchmark appears to be a genuine, still-open gap rather than a crowded space — but this project's own Problem Definition doc reaches the same conclusion independently, so this is a converging rather than novel finding.

### Cited Findings
- F1TENTH gym (O'Kelly et al., NeurIPS 2019 Competition Track / PMLR 123, 2020) and MicroRacer (Asperti & Del Brutto, arXiv:2203.10494, LOD 2022) are lightweight, open, Gymnasium-compatible racing environments with published PPO/SAC/TD3 baselines, but per this project's own Problem Definition doc (`D:\ai-racing-bot\PROBLEM_DEFINITION.md`), neither is built around a cross-track held-out generalization protocol as its primary scored metric — [F1TENTH gym paper]; [MicroRacer arXiv:2203.10494].
- The Learn-to-Race environment/challenge is the clearest existing prior art for a generalization-scored racing benchmark, but L2R uses a high-fidelity (Arrival-derived) simulator, not a lightweight 2D one, and is oriented toward "safe learning" (constraint violations, safety metrics) as much as generalization — [Learn-to-Race Challenge 2022 paper (arXiv:2205.02953)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.02953); [learn-to-race.org](https://learn-to-race.org/).
- A broader RL-generalization position paper (arXiv:2412.16970, "A Research Agenda for Usability and Generalisation in Reinforcement Learning") argues that most current generalization research is fragmented across bespoke, engineer-authored simulators, each requiring significant programming expertise, and that this fragmentation itself is a barrier to progress on RL generalization broadly — [arXiv:2412.16970](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.16970).
- A recent RL-evaluation critique (arXiv:2510.10541, "Rethinking RL Evaluation: Can Benchmarks Truly Reveal Failures of RL Methods?") reports that on some current RL benchmarks, "an RL model trained on a benchmark's training split scores about the same as an RL model trained directly on the test split," i.e., the held-out set fails to actually test generalization — a general benchmark-design failure mode directly relevant to how rigorously any new cross-track benchmark should define its held-out set — [arXiv:2510.10541](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10541).

### Inferences
- Because the one benchmark most similar in spirit (L2R) is heavyweight and appears to have gone quiet as an active challenge, and the lightweight environments (F1TENTH gym, MicroRacer, CarRacing) don't foreground cross-track generalization as their scored metric, there is a plausible, currently-open niche for exactly what this project's Problem Definition proposes: a lightweight, reproducible, explicitly generalization-scored benchmark. This is evidence *for* staying narrowly focused on racing rather than expanding scope, since the specific niche has not been "solved out from under" the project.
- The benchmark-design critique above (train/test leakage risk) is a direct, actionable risk for this specific project's Phase 2/6 plan (procedural track generation + held-out evaluation) — the team should be able to show its held-out tracks are meaningfully distinct (which the Implementation Plan already flags as a diversity-metric requirement), or risk building a benchmark with the same flaw being called out in current RL-evaluation literature.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence was found of any other team currently building "a lightweight open cross-track generalization racing benchmark" in 2024–2026 outside of what's cited above; absence of evidence is not proof no one else is doing this, just that a reasonably thorough search did not surface a competing effort.
- Could not verify whether Learn-to-Race's codebase/challenge has been revived, forked, or extended by any other group since 2023.

## What has worked in other domains sharing the same abstract structure (generalize a control policy across procedurally varied environment instances), and is one of them a notably better testbed than racing?

### Takeaway
Legged locomotion over randomized terrain (ETH Zurich-style) and procedurally-generated game-level benchmarks (Procgen, Crafter/Craftax, NetHack) are the most mature, most-cited communities working on exactly this abstract problem, and on pure engineering/iteration-speed grounds several of them (especially Craftax/Procgen-style GPU-native benchmarks) are objectively cheaper and faster to iterate on than a physics-based racing sim — but they are not doing the same *specific* thing (vehicle dynamics + track-geometry generalization), so "better testbed for generalization research in the abstract" does not mean "better testbed for this project's specific research question."

### Cited Findings
- **Legged locomotion / terrain randomization**: ETH Zurich's Robotic Systems Lab's `legged_gym` (built on NVIDIA Isaac Gym) trains quadrupeds (ANYmal) to walk on procedurally varied rough terrain (smooth slope, rough slope, stairs up/down, wave, discrete obstacles) with a terrain-difficulty curriculum, plus actuator-network modeling, friction/mass randomization, and observation noise for sim-to-real transfer — a very close structural analog to this project's track/physics/sensor generalization framing — [leggedrobotics/legged_gym GitHub](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym); [README](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym/blob/master/README.md).
- **Procedurally-generated game benchmarks**: Procgen (Cobbe et al., ICML 2020, arXiv:1912.01588) is described as "the canonical benchmark for studying generalization in RL," using 16 environments with procedurally generated levels specifically so agents are evaluated on unseen levels; a NeurIPS 2020 Procgen competition ran via AIcrowd — [OpenAI Procgen](https://openai.com/index/procgen-benchmark/); [Procgen paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.01588); [Measuring Sample Efficiency/Generalization: NeurIPS 2020 Procgen Benchmark, arXiv:2103.15332](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.15332).
- **Craftax** (arXiv:2402.16801) reimplements the Crafter benchmark in JAX and reports enabling "billions of environment interactions on a single GPU in under an hour" by eliminating CPU–GPU transfer bottlenecks — a concrete, citable claim that this class of benchmark can be dramatically cheaper/faster to iterate on than most physics-simulator-based RL tasks — [Craftax paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.16801).
- **NetHack Learning Environment** (Küttler et al., NeurIPS 2020) is explicitly procedurally generated per-episode "to test the generalization limits of current state-of-the-art approaches," and is described as having become "a central benchmark" for sequential decision-making research, with large human/bot datasets integrated — [NLE paper](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper/2020/file/569ff987c643b4bedf504efda8f786c2-Paper.pdf); [Meta AI blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/nethack-learning-environment-to-advance-deep-reinforcement-learning/).
- **Drone racing/navigation with randomized obstacles**: multiple 2024–2026 papers (arXiv:2411.04246 "Learning Generalizable Policy for Obstacle-Aware Autonomous Drone Racing"; arXiv:2606.27348 "Bridging Performance and Generalization in Reinforcement Learning for Agile Flight") report domain randomization over track/obstacle configuration combined with curriculum learning to achieve zero-shot generalization to unseen cluttered environments, with drones reaching speeds up to 70 km/h in unseen environments — this is essentially the same "cross-track generalization" research question as this project's, just in 3D free flight rather than 2D ground vehicles — [arXiv:2411.04246](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04246); [arXiv:2606.27348](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27348).
- **Warehouse/logistics robot navigation**: RWARE (Robot Warehouse) is a cited multi-agent RL environment for shelf-fetching/logistics; recent papers (arXiv:2411.06128, "warehouse robot navigation algorithm in complex warehouse layout") test generalization across "complex warehouse layouts" and note four key desiderata for real-world robot navigation RL: reasoning under uncertainty, safety, learning from limited data, and generalization to diverse environments — [arXiv:2411.06128](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2411.06128).
- A generalization survey (arXiv:2401.02349, "A Survey Analyzing Generalization in Deep Reinforcement Learning") notes that "most benchmarks focus on either video games or robotics tasks such as locomotion and manipulation, covering only a limited set of application domains, and it remains unclear whether algorithmic improvements generalize beyond the specific domains on which they are evaluated" — a direct citable caution against assuming results from one generalization-testbed domain (e.g., Procgen) transfer to another (e.g., racing) — [arXiv:2401.02349].

### Inferences
- On pure infrastructure/iteration-speed grounds, JAX-native procedural-game benchmarks (Craftax/Procgen-style) are almost certainly cheaper and faster to run at scale than any physics-based vehicle simulator, including this project's own 2D racing sim — this is a real cost argument if the team's goal were purely "fastest path to a generalization result," but it is not evidence that racing is a *worse* domain for this specific project's stated question (vehicle-dynamics-and-track generalization has domain-specific value distinct from grid-world/game generalization).
- Legged-locomotion terrain randomization is the closest "adjacent domain" structurally (procedural environment variation + a swappable physics/sensor stack + curriculum), and it is a mature, well-funded, actively-published research community (ETH Zurich RSL and its extensive downstream citation base) — if the team ever wanted to generalize the *type* of contribution rather than the specific vehicle domain, legged locomotion is the most credible adjacent direction based on current evidence, more so than warehouse logistics or general game-playing.
- Drone racing/navigation is arguably the single closest domain to "the same problem, different embodiment" — it already frames itself with the same vocabulary (racing, procedural obstacle courses, zero-shot generalization to unseen environments) — meaning this project's core research question is not unique to ground-vehicle racing, and adjacent-domain researchers are already validating that domain randomization + curriculum learning is a workable path to cross-environment generalization in a racing-shaped task.

### Gaps
- Could not find a direct, apples-to-apples cost/compute comparison (e.g., wall-clock GPU-hours to reach a fixed generalization benchmark score) between a 2D racing sim like this project's and Procgen/Craftax/legged_gym; the "cheaper to iterate" claim for Craftax is well-sourced in isolation but no source directly benchmarks it against a vehicle-dynamics racing sim.
- Could not find citable evidence on whether the legged-locomotion or drone-racing communities view their own domain as "solved" for generalization or still substantially open — the papers found are presenting new methods/results (2024-2026), implying it's still active, but no explicit "state of the field" retrospective was found for either.

## What do researchers/organizers/commentators say about racing's value vs. limitations as an RL generalization testbed?

### Takeaway
The available commentary is broadly favorable to racing as a testbed — framing it as a "proving ground," "microcosm," or "litmus test" for broader autonomous-driving and control research — but the more rigorous, paper-level sources are more circumspect: even Sony AI's own follow-on work on GT Sophy explicitly names unseen-situation generalization as "a remaining frontier," and no source found argues racing is a "toy" or dismisses it as a low-value domain; the main documented limitation is that racing competitions to date mostly measure known-track performance, not held-out generalization.

### Cited Findings
- General framing found in press/community sources: "autonomous racing is a testbed where competition spurs innovation... AI-controlled cars racing to the finish line chart a course toward safer autonomous vehicles" and "when autonomous systems can reliably function in these extreme conditions, they inherently possess a buffer when operating in the ordinary conditions of street traffic" — [Inverse: "How Autonomous Racing Is Pushing Self-Driving Cars Forward"](https://www.inverse.com/science/self-driving-cars-ai-autonomous-racing).
- Academic framing: F1TENTH is described as "an exciting testbed for cutting-edge robotics and autonomous vehicle research, encompassing diverse aspects of autonomous systems including perception, planning, control, and learning, with a focus on adversarial and high-speed environments" — [Unifying F1TENTH Autonomous Racing: Survey, Methods and Benchmarks, arXiv:2402.18558](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.18558).
- The Betz et al. survey already cited in this project's own Problem Definition doc (IEEE OJITS, 2022; arXiv:2202.07008) names low generalizability to unseen tracks and large data requirements of end-to-end/RL methods as core open weaknesses of the autonomous-racing field — i.e., a survey-level, citable statement that generalization specifically (not racing generally) is the open problem, reinforcing that racing is being treated by its own survey literature as a legitimate, unsolved research area rather than a solved toy domain (already in `D:\ai-racing-bot\PROBLEM_DEFINITION.md`, §2, and re-confirmed in this research pass).
- Sony AI's own newer publication on GT Sophy (arXiv:2504.09021) is a citable acknowledgment, from the team behind the most prominent racing-RL success story, that generalizing/adapting to new situations without retraining "is a remaining frontier" — i.e., even the highest-profile success in this space treats cross-situation generalization as still open, not solved — [arXiv:2504.09021](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09021).
- No source found in this research pass explicitly argued that racing is a "toy" domain or a poor proxy for control-generalization research; the closest thing to a limitation claim found is structural (competition rules measuring known-track performance, not held-out generalization — see first section above) rather than a stated opinion that racing itself is a weak domain.

### Inferences
- The absence of any found "racing is a niche/toy domain" critique, combined with multiple survey- and publication-level sources treating racing-RL generalization as a genuinely open problem, is modest positive evidence that staying in racing is defensible on its own merits — the team doesn't appear to need to expand scope just to escape a domain seen as low-value by the field.
- The gap between competition-level framing ("proving ground for safer self-driving") and the more precise academic framing (generalization specifically still open) suggests any external communication about this project should be careful to say "cross-track generalization is the open question," not "racing generally is unsolved" — the latter is not well-supported and the former is exactly what the Learn-to-Race Challenge already demonstrated with concrete numbers.

### Gaps
- Could not find a dedicated position paper or retrospective specifically titled/framed as "is racing a good RL testbed" — the supportive framing found is dispersed across press articles, survey introductions, and competition marketing rather than a single authoritative source, and no rebuttal/skeptical source was found despite searching for one; this may reflect a real absence of published skepticism, or may reflect search limitations. Note this explicitly as unresolved rather than treating the favorable framing as the final word.

## Concrete cost of extending this specific project toward (a) real-world-style driving, (b) a different but related domain (e.g., terrain generalization), or (c) broadening within racing

### Takeaway
Based on the project's own architecture (read directly from `PROBLEM_DEFINITION.md` and `IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md`), option (c) — broadening within racing (multi-agent tactics, race strategy) — is the cheapest extension because it reuses every existing module (physics, track, sensors, env) unchanged and only adds new agent/strategy logic on top; option (b) — a different domain sharing the same abstract structure (e.g., generalizing across terrain types for a ground robot) — would require replacing the `VehiclePhysics` and `Track` implementations but could keep the `Sensor` protocol and the overall Gymnasium/eval-harness architecture, making it a moderate-cost pivot; option (a) — real-world-style driving with traffic/pedestrians/road networks — is the most expensive by a wide margin because it breaks the project's own core simplifying assumptions (single-vehicle, closed-loop track, no dynamic agents/rules-of-the-road) and would require essentially new interfaces beyond the existing three protocols.

### Cited Findings
- The project's own Problem Definition (`D:\ai-racing-bot\PROBLEM_DEFINITION.md`, §5) explicitly reserves multi-agent racing tactics and race strategy (fuel, tire wear, pit-stop timing) as "later chapters," citing GT Sophy (Wurman et al., *Nature* 602:223–228, 2022) as the evidenced precedent for multi-agent tactics being a distinct, real research area layered on top of single-agent racing — i.e., broadening within racing already has a citable roadmap and precedent (source: local project doc).
- The Implementation Plan's own architecture table (`D:\ai-racing-bot\IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md`, §2) shows the existing modularity is organized exactly around `VehiclePhysics`, `Track`, and `Sensor` protocols, with the Gymnasium environment (`ARS-Racing-v0`) as the integration point; extending "within racing" (multi-agent, strategy) adds new agent-level and reward-level logic without touching these three core protocols, while extending to a materially different environment (open road network with traffic) would require new entities (other vehicles/pedestrians with their own policies or scripted behavior, right-of-way/traffic-rule logic, a road-network representation rather than a single closed loop) that do not fit the current `Track` Protocol's `query()/sample_at_s()/is_on_track()` interface, which is designed for a single loop, not a branching network (source: local project doc, `IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md` §5, Phase 2 task list).
- The closest published analog for option (b) — legged locomotion generalizing across randomized terrain — reuses a nearly identical conceptual split (procedurally varied environment + swappable actuator/physics model + noisy sensors + curriculum), per ETH Zurich's `legged_gym`, suggesting that if this project pivoted to "generalize across terrain types for a simple ground robot," the existing `VehiclePhysics`/`Track`/`Sensor` protocol split would likely still be a reasonable architecture — the new work would be concentrated in building new physics (traction models per terrain type) and new "track" (terrain) generators, while sensors and the Gym env/eval harness could largely carry over — [leggedrobotics/legged_gym](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym).
- For option (a), the F1TENTH ICRA rules already show that even *simple* multi-vehicle interaction (2-car head-to-head, or obstacles unknown at run time) required F1TENTH's organizers to build separate qualification/race-mode logic and explicit anti-exploit code review — [F1TENTH ICRA 2023 Rules](https://icra2023-race.f1tenth.org/rules.html) — suggesting that adding dynamic other-agents (traffic/pedestrians) to this project would non-trivially increase the environment's complexity and the evaluation harness's complexity (Phase 6 in the Implementation Plan currently assumes single-policy, single-track metrics like lap time and generalization gap; multi-agent/traffic scenarios would need entirely new metrics such as collision rates with dynamic agents, right-of-way compliance, etc., none of which are designed into the current Phase 6 plan).

### Inferences
- Because the current architecture's three protocols are purpose-built around a single vehicle on a single closed track, the *engineering* cost ordering is almost certainly (c) broadening within racing < (b) different domain, same abstract structure < (a) real-world driving — this is an architectural inference from reading the project's own docs, not from an external source, and should be flagged to the team as such (a design-fit judgment, not a market/community finding).
- The Implementation Plan's explicit gating structure (Phase 0 → 1/2 → 3/4 → 5 → 6 → 7) is itself evidence that the team has already sequenced its resources around finishing the narrow cross-track generalization question first; any scope expansion before Phase 6/7 land would directly compete with the dependency chain the team has already committed to, independent of which expansion direction is chosen.

### Gaps
- No external cost/effort benchmarks were found for "cost of adding traffic/pedestrians to a 2D racing sim" or "cost of pivoting a vehicle-racing RL codebase to legged locomotion" — this section's findings are primarily an architectural reading of the project's own current-state documents, cross-referenced against how comparable systems (legged_gym, F1TENTH) are structured, not a market study of engineering-hours estimates. Treat the effort-ordering as a well-reasoned inference, not a measured fact.

## Are there citable arguments that narrowly-scoped domains (like racing) are preferable for rigorous, publishable generalization results, versus broader domains where confounds are harder to control?

### Takeaway
No single position paper was found arguing explicitly "narrow domains like racing are preferable to broad domains for generalization rigor," but there is convergent, citable evidence for the underlying logic: (1) a recent RL-evaluation critique shows that poorly-controlled held-out sets can silently fail to test generalization at all, which is easier to catch and control in a narrow, parameterized domain; (2) this project's own Problem Definition already makes exactly this argument implicitly, citing the value of controlled, independently-swappable variables (track distribution, physics fidelity, sensor modality) for isolating confounds — a design logic that gets structurally harder to preserve as domain breadth increases (e.g., adding traffic introduces confounds like other-agent policy variation that are hard to hold constant across conditions).

### Cited Findings
- arXiv:2510.10541 ("Rethinking RL Evaluation: Can Benchmarks Truly Reveal Failures of RL Methods?") demonstrates empirically that on some existing benchmarks, training directly on the "held-out" test split produces performance indistinguishable from proper training, meaning the benchmark's generalization claim was not actually being tested — a direct, citable illustration of how easy it is for a generalization benchmark to be broken by insufficient control over what varies between train and test, which is a stronger risk in more complex/broader domains with more uncontrolled factors — [arXiv:2510.10541](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10541).
- arXiv:2401.02349 ("A Survey Analyzing Generalization in Deep Reinforcement Learning") states that because most current benchmarks cover "only a limited set of application domains," "it remains unclear whether algorithmic improvements generalize beyond the specific domains on which they are evaluated" — this cuts both ways: it is a citable caution against assuming a narrow-domain (racing) result generalizes to claims about RL generalization broadly, but it is equally a caution against a broad-domain benchmark being so heterogeneous that no clean causal claim about *what* enabled generalization can be made — [arXiv:2401.02349](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.02349).
- This project's own Problem Definition doc (`D:\ai-racing-bot\PROBLEM_DEFINITION.md`, §3) makes an internal, project-specific version of the "narrow domain enables rigor" argument directly: it argues the modular `VehiclePhysics`/`Track`/`Sensor` protocol split lets track distribution, physics fidelity, and observation modality "vary independently and be swapped without rebuilding the rest of the stack," which is presented as necessary "so a generalization result isn't confounded by an artificial physics ceiling" (citing Brunnbauer et al.'s finding that generalization sensitivity depends on observation model choice) — this is the team's own citable rationale for why a controlled, narrow domain is methodologically preferable for this specific research question (source: local project doc, not an independent external source).

### Inferences
- The strongest available argument for staying narrow is not a general position paper (none was found) but a compositional one: (a) generalization benchmarks are demonstrably easy to break through insufficient train/test control (arXiv:2510.10541), and (b) a narrow, modular domain such as this project's racing sim makes it easier to enforce that control (independently swappable track/physics/sensor variables, per the project's own architecture) than a broader domain would, where additional dimensions of variation (e.g., other agents' behavior policies, road-network topology, traffic-rule variants) are much harder to hold constant or even enumerate. This is a reasoned synthesis across sources, not a single citable claim — flagged accordingly for the report writer to present as an inference, not a quoted fact.
- No source was found that frames this as an explicit trade-off debate ("narrow vs. broad domains for RL rigor") — this appears to be an underexplored meta-question in the literature searched, rather than a settled one either way.
