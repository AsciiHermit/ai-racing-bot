# Adjacent RL/Robotics Generalization & Sim-to-Real Techniques (Non-Racing-Specific)

## What does the broader generalization-in-deep-RL literature identify as the most effective technique families for OOD generalization, and which apply to low-dimensional continuous control (driving) rather than pixel-based games?

### Takeaway
The dominant, best-evidenced families are (1) environment/data diversity — procedural content generation and domain randomization — and (2) representation-level regularization (data augmentation, dropout/L2/batch-norm, and randomized-network perturbation). Most of the strongest quantitative evidence comes from pixel-based benchmarks (Procgen, CoinRun) where regularization interacts with a convolutional visual encoder; for a low-dimensional continuous-control task like 2D driving (state vectors, not pixels), the visual-specific augmentation results (random crop, network randomization on conv layers) are *not directly transferable* — the applicable subset is: increasing environment/level diversity, generic regularization (L2, dropout) on the policy/value MLPs, and explicit train/test set separation methodology, not image-space augmentation.

### Cited Findings
- Cobbe et al., "Quantifying Generalization in Reinforcement Learning," introduced CoinRun/Procgen-style train/test splits and found agents "strongly overfit to small training sets in almost all Procgen environments," with some environments requiring as many as 10,000 training levels to close the generalization gap — evidence that raw environment/level diversity is a first-order lever. — [Quantifying Generalization in RL (arXiv:1812.02341)](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02341v3)
- In the CoinRun study, L2 regularization, dropout, data augmentation, and batch normalization (standard supervised-learning regularizers) all measurably reduced the generalization gap; deeper convolutional architectures also generalized better. — [Quantifying Generalization in RL](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/cobbe19a/cobbe19a.pdf)
- Random-crop data augmentation (as in RAD/DrAC) improved generalization on CoinRun/Procgen; "appropriate augmentations are beneficial in almost every environment, with explicit regularization leading to further improvements." — [Automatic Data Augmentation for Generalization in RL](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.12862v2)
- Network Randomization (Lee et al., ICLR 2020) inserts a randomized (untrained) conv layer to perturb visual input at training time, producing features more invariant to unseen visual domains; validated on 2D CoinRun, 3D DeepMind Lab, and 3D robotics control tasks. — [Network Randomization (arXiv:1910.05396)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.05396)
- Kirk, Zhang, Grefenstette & Rocktäschel's survey (arXiv:2111.09794, published JAIR 2023) explicitly cautions that "taking a purely procedural content generation approach to benchmark design is not conducive to progress in [zero-shot generalization]" and calls out fast online adaptation, offline-RL generalization, and reward-function-variation benchmarks as underexplored future-work areas. — [A Survey of Zero-shot Generalisation in Deep RL (arXiv:2111.09794)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09794)

### Inferences
- For the team's 2D racing simulator (low-dimensional state, not pixels), the transferable lessons from this literature are: (a) train-set diversity/size matters more than any single algorithmic trick — i.e., the number and variety of randomized track instances used in training is likely a bigger lever than the choice of RL algorithm; (b) standard MLP regularization (dropout, L2, weight decay, batch/layer norm) is cheap, well-evidenced, and should be tried as a baseline improvement on top of domain randomization; (c) image-space tricks (random crop, randomized conv layers) are not applicable since the observation space is presumably vectorized (position, velocity, sensor rays, track curvature), not pixels — the team should not spend effort porting these.
- Kirk et al.'s critique of "PCG-only" benchmark design is a direct methodological warning: simply randomizing track parameters (which the team's plan already does) may produce agents that generalize within the randomization distribution but not to structurally different held-out layouts unless the *train* distribution of track geometries is itself diverse in structure (curvature sequences, topology), not just in scalar parameters (width, friction, etc.).

### Gaps
- Found no study directly benchmarking regularization techniques (dropout/L2/augmentation) on a *low-dimensional vector-observation* continuous-control task analogous to driving; the strong evidence base is specifically visual/pixel-based (Procgen/CoinRun/DeepMind Lab). Extrapolating regularization benefits to vector-input MLP policies is a plausible but unverified inference.
- Could not find a direct quantitative comparison of "PCG-only" vs. "PCG + structural/topological diversity" training regimes for driving-like tasks specifically.

## What robotics sim-to-real techniques (domain randomization variants, system identification/online adaptation, RMA-style rapid motor adaptation, teacher-student distillation, dynamics randomization + privileged learning) have been used for legged robots/drones/ground vehicles, and could they transfer to procedurally-varying racetracks?

### Takeaway
The single most transferable idea from legged-robot sim-to-real research is the **privileged-teacher / adaptation-module (RMA) pattern**: train a policy with privileged information unavailable at deployment (e.g., true track curvature ahead, true tire friction), then either (a) distill a student that only sees deployment-realistic observations, or (b) train a small online module to infer the privileged/latent context from a short history of proprioceptive-style observations. This is well-evidenced in legged robotics and is architecturally lightweight enough for a small team to implement in a 2D simulator (it does not require real hardware — only a simulator that can expose privileged state during training).

### Cited Findings
- RMA (Kumar et al., 2021) trains a base policy conditioned on privileged environmental information (terrain, friction, mass), then trains an adaptation module to infer a compressed representation of that privileged info online from a history of proprioceptive states/actions; it is "trained completely in simulation without... predefined foot trajectory generators and is deployed... without any fine-tuning." — [RMA: Rapid Motor Adaptation for Legged Robots (arXiv:2107.04034)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.04034)
- RMA "consistently outperformed baseline methods (fixed policies, domain randomization, system identification, AWR) across metrics such as success rate, time to failure, and distance covered" on a real A1 quadruped across diverse terrains and payloads up to 12 kg. — [RMA overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2107.04034)
- Teacher-student / privileged-learning distillation is described generally as: "distilling a state-privileged teacher into a student policy that operates from partial observations... simulators provide access to privileged information, including ground-truth state, that is unavailable in the real world," and has been applied to quadruped perceptive locomotion, blind/depth-conditioned locomotion, drone flight, and simulated autonomous driving. — [Sim2Real teacher-student survey discussion, search synthesis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10014)
- TWIST distills a privileged, state-based "teacher" world model into a vision-based student world model for efficient sim-to-real transfer of model-based RL — i.e., the privileged/student split can be applied at the *world-model* level, not just the policy level, which is directly relevant to a Dreamer-style agent. — [TWIST project page](https://twist-sim2real.github.io/)
- On domain-randomization variants: Yu et al. (2019, cited in review) "intertwined policy optimization, system identification, and domain randomization... with the policy conditioned on a latent space projection of domain parameters" — i.e., instead of blind randomization, the policy is explicitly conditioned on an inferred embedding of the current dynamics/track parameters, which online system identification estimates from rollout history. — [Robot Learning from Randomized Simulations: A Review (arXiv:2111.00956)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.00956)
- BayRnTune proposes adaptive Bayesian domain randomization with strategic fine-tuning rather than fixed randomization ranges, updating the randomization distribution based on transfer performance. — [BayRnTune (arXiv:2310.10606)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.10606)
- AdaptSim performs task-driven adaptation of the simulation *itself* (not just the policy) to better match target performance, an alternative to static domain randomization ranges. — [AdaptSim (arXiv:2302.04903)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.04903)
- "Not Only Domain Randomization: Universal Policy with Embedding System Identification" explicitly frames plain DR as insufficient alone and proposes conditioning a "universal policy" on an embedding produced by an online system-identification module — architecturally close to RMA. — [arXiv:2109.13438](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.13438)

### Inferences
- For the racing project, the RMA pattern maps naturally onto track generalization: treat track-instance parameters (curvature profile, surface friction, width) as the "privileged terrain info," train a base policy that sees them during training, and either distill a student that only uses onboard-sensor-realistic observations, or train a fast online adaptation module that infers a latent track-context embedding from a short window of recent sensor/vehicle-state history. This directly targets zero-shot generalization to unseen geometries, since the adaptation module — not the policy — is what has to generalize, and adaptation modules trained this way have repeatedly outperformed plain domain randomization and system identification baselines in the legged-robot literature.
- This is comparatively cheap for a small team to implement relative to UED methods (see next section): it requires (a) a simulator that can expose ground-truth track/dynamics parameters during training (the team's modular Track/VehiclePhysics components likely already have this), and (b) a second small network (adaptation module) trained via supervised regression or via RL fine-tuning after the base policy is frozen — no adversarial curriculum generator, no additional environment-generation infrastructure.
- Given the team is already planning a Dreamer-style (model-based) agent, the TWIST-style privileged-teacher-world-model → student-world-model distillation is a natural extension: train the world model with privileged track state during training and distill/adapt it toward the sensor-limited case, rather than only applying privilege at the policy layer.

### Gaps
- Found no papers applying RMA/privileged-distillation specifically to *procedurally varying track geometry* for ground vehicles (as opposed to terrain roughness/friction for legged locomotion or payload variation) — the geometric-variation case (unseen curvature sequences, not just unseen surface properties) is not directly evidenced in this literature; it's an inference that the same architecture would help, not a verified result.
- Could not verify quantitative comparisons of RMA-style adaptation vs. domain randomization specifically for wheeled/vehicle dynamics (all strong RMA quantitative results found are quadruped-specific, on real A1 hardware).

## What role do foundation models / large pretrained world models play in recent (2023–2026) generalist control research — is there evidence they help with structural/geometric generalization specifically (vs. visual/appearance generalization)?

### Takeaway
Foundation-model/world-model research in this window is heavily concentrated on visual/semantic generalization (novel objects, textures, language-conditioned tasks) via large-scale pretraining on video/robot-manipulation data; there is comparatively little direct, verified evidence that this class of approach specifically improves *geometric/structural* generalization (e.g., to novel spatial layouts or novel track topologies) as opposed to appearance generalization. The "world-action model" (WAM) framing — jointly predicting future video and action, inheriting physics priors from video pretraining — is the closest conceptual link to structural generalization, but the claims found are largely qualitative/aspirational rather than quantitatively isolating structural vs. visual generalization.

### Cited Findings
- Survey framing: robotic policy learning is "rapidly shifting toward foundation-model-driven embodied intelligence," with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies mapping multimodal observations directly to actions, but "purely reactive VLA policies remain limited in complex physical environments, where they often struggle with long-horizon reasoning, temporal credit assignment, and robustness under compounding errors." — [World Model for Robot Learning: A Comprehensive Survey (arXiv:2605.00080)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00080v1)
- "World Action Models" (WAMs), which jointly predict video and action, are claimed to "inherit world physics priors that enable effective learning from diverse, non-repetitive data, open-world generalization, cross-embodiment learning from video-only data, and few-shot adaptation to new robots." — [World Action Models are Zero-shot Policies (arXiv:2602.15922)](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15922v1)
- Foundation-model-for-manipulation surveys frame affordance/generalization along "geometric, visual, semantic, and multimodal" axes and note that "geometric affordance theory posits that an object's functional possibilities are determined by its 3D shape, structure, and kinematics," with recent work inferring part-level geometry directly from 3D observations — i.e., geometric generalization is treated as a distinct axis from visual/appearance generalization, but the cited work is about object-level manipulation affordances, not spatial/track-layout generalization. — [Transferring Foundation Models for Generalizable Robotic Manipulation (arXiv:2306.05716)](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05716)
- A limitation explicitly noted across this literature: current foundation-model approaches "rely on collecting large-scale robotic data which is costly and time-consuming, and due to insufficient diversity of data, they typically suffer from limiting their capability in open-domain scenarios with new objects and diverse environments." — [Transferring Foundation Models for Generalizable Robotic Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05716)

### Inferences
- No evidence was found that pretraining on internet-scale video/robot data specifically confers an advantage for *geometric/topological* generalization (e.g., generalizing to unseen racetrack layouts) as distinct from visual-appearance generalization (textures, lighting, object identity). The claims of "open-world generalization" for WAMs are stated at a high level without an isolated structural-generalization ablation in the sources found.
- Given the team's simulator is 2D, low-dimensional, and non-visual, large pretrained video/vision-language-action foundation models are very likely **not** a cost-effective path — the entire value proposition of these models (transferring visual/semantic priors from internet-scale image/video data) is inapplicable when there are no images/pixels in the loop. This is a case where a broad literature trend should probably be explicitly *rejected* for this project rather than adapted.
- If the team wants a "generalist" flavor of technique at low cost, the TD-MPC2 approach (a single scalable world-model architecture trained across many tasks/embodiments with task-agnostic architectural tricks — SimNorm, learnable task embeddings, action masking) is a more proportionate adjacent idea than VLA-style foundation models: it stays within reinforcement learning and model-based control (consistent with the team's Dreamer-style direction) while demonstrating that one architecture/hyperparameter set can generalize across 80+ tasks in simulation. — [TD-MPC2 (arXiv:2310.16828)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16828)

### Gaps
- Could not find any paper that isolates "structural/geometric" from "visual/appearance" generalization in a controlled ablation for foundation-model-based control; this is an important open question the report should flag as unresolved rather than settled.
- Could not verify specific quantitative claims for OCTO, DreamZero, or other named generalist policies mentioned in search snippets — these appeared only in aggregated search summaries, not primary sources fetched directly, so specific performance numbers should not be treated as confirmed.

## What does offline RL / RL-from-demonstration research suggest about using classical-optimal-control trajectories (e.g., minimum-curvature racing lines) as auxiliary training signal or reward shaping?

### Takeaway
There is solid, generalizable evidence (outside racing) that using an automated optimal-control expert (e.g., MPC) to generate demonstration trajectories, then using those demonstrations to bootstrap RL via offline-to-online training and/or potential-based reward shaping, substantially improves sample efficiency and can let the learned policy *exceed* the expert's performance — this pattern should transfer well to using minimum-curvature racing-line trajectories as an auxiliary signal.

### Cited Findings
- Potential-based reward shaping (PBRS) is "a mathematically principled mechanism... for accelerating policy learning in sparse or challenging reward environments," with a core guarantee: "if the shaping reward is constructed appropriately, the set of optimal policies remains unchanged (policy invariance)." — [Potential-Based Reward Shaping overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/potential-based-reward-shaping)
- "Reward Shaping from Demonstrations" (RSfD) encodes demonstration trajectories directly as a potential-based shaping function when it is non-trivial to hand-design a potential function — directly applicable to using a minimum-curvature racing line's state-action trace as the potential source instead of a hand-crafted shaping term. — [search synthesis, PBRS literature](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/policy-invariant-reward-shaping-pbrs)
- In MuJoCo continuous-control benchmarks (Hopper, Swimmer, Humanoid), a potential-based approach achieved "an average increase of 23.5% in episode rewards... compared with state-of-the-art approaches" — quantitative evidence of the general effectiveness of shaping in continuous control (not racing-specific, but same problem class: continuous-control locomotion with dense dynamics). — [search synthesis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.08823)
- A directly analogous pattern to "use classical optimal control as a teacher": a 2026 paper (arXiv:2608.12063) uses **Sampling-Based Model Predictive Control (SMPC)** as an automated expert, solving tasks with dense, easily-tuned cost functions in simulation to generate demonstration data at "one million samples per hour" via GPU-parallel environments, then trains a modified TD3 agent that starts with 50% expert transitions in its replay buffer and phases them out via curriculum once the agent hits 10% success rate on sparse task rewards. Learned policies "substantially outperform the SMPC teacher": task completion speed improved by more than 50% on some tasks, task-duration variance dropped 11–45%, and success rates approached 100% in simulation; critically, "policies trained exclusively on sparse rewards completely fail without adding expert SMPC data." — [Learning Loco-Manipulation From SMPC Demonstrations With Sparse Offline-to-Online RL (arXiv:2608.12063)](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.12063)
- The authors of that paper frame the core idea generally as bypassing "the RL reward-tuning bottleneck" by using model-based optimal control entirely in simulation as an automated offline teacher, "while allowing RL to outperform the expert demonstrations" — the mechanism (MPC/optimal-control expert → offline-to-online RL bootstrap) is explicitly stated as more general than the loco-manipulation tasks it was tested on. — [same source](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.12063)

### Inferences
- This directly supports the racing team's likely intuition: a minimum-curvature (or minimum-time) racing line computed by classical optimal control for each procedurally generated track could serve as (a) a potential-based reward-shaping signal (keep policy invariance, speed up learning) or (b) offline-to-online demonstration data seeded into the replay buffer, with a curriculum that phases out the demonstrations as the RL agent's own success rate rises — mirroring the SMPC paper's approach almost exactly, just swapping "MPC for loco-manipulation" for "minimum-curvature solver for racing lines."
- Because minimum-curvature racing lines can be computed cheaply and automatically for *any* procedurally generated track (unlike hand-crafted human demonstrations, which don't scale to randomized geometries), this approach specifically solves the scaling problem that would otherwise block demonstration-based methods in a domain-randomization setting — the "teacher" is itself procedurally regenerable per-track.
- The quantitative pattern found ("RL policies trained with demonstration bootstrapping can exceed the demonstrator, and pure sparse-reward RL can completely fail without it") suggests that if the team's sparse/lap-time-based reward is difficult to optimize directly, expert-trajectory bootstrapping is a well-evidenced fix, not merely a nice-to-have.

### Gaps
- All strong quantitative evidence found for MPC-as-teacher/offline-to-online bootstrapping is from loco-manipulation (whole-body robot control), not driving or racing; no source directly measures this technique's effect on *generalization to unseen track geometries* specifically (as opposed to sample efficiency/final performance on trained tasks). The generalization benefit is a plausible extrapolation, not a confirmed finding.
- Could not find a source directly discussing potential-based reward shaping combined with domain-randomized/procedurally-varying environments (i.e., whether shaping signals derived from a per-instance optimal solution help or hurt cross-instance generalization, versus just speeding up training on each instance).

## Are there notable multi-task or unsupervised-environment-design (UED) methods (PAIRED, ACCEL, robust adversarial RL, minimax-regret curricula) with quantitative evidence of improved generalization to held-out instances, and how expensive/complex are they relative to plain domain randomization?

### Takeaway
UED methods (PAIRED, ACCEL, and successors) are a well-established, actively-developed family with repeated qualitative/quantitative claims of superior zero-shot transfer to challenging held-out environments compared to uniform domain randomization, but they are substantially more complex to implement than plain DR — they require training an additional adversary/generator agent (or an evolutionary level-mutation loop) alongside the main policy, plus a regret-estimation mechanism, and the field itself is still iterating on making these methods stable and computationally tractable (multiple 2023–2026 papers exist specifically to fix instability and inefficiency in the original methods).

### Cited Findings
- PAIRED (Protagonist Antagonist Induced Regret Environment Design, NeurIPS 2020) trains an adversarial "teacher" to generate levels that maximize the *regret* (return gap) between a protagonist and an antagonist agent, rather than simply maximizing difficulty; regret is defined as "the difference between the agent's return and the return of the optimal policy." This produces "a curriculum of difficult but achievable environments tailored to the agents' current level of performance" and is reported to facilitate "zero-shot transfer to challenging new environments at test time." — [PAIRED (Google Research blog / NeurIPS 2020 paper)](http://aima.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/papers/neurips20-paired.pdf), [research.google blog](https://research.google/blog/paired-a-new-multi-agent-approach-for-adversarial-environment-generation/)
- ACCEL (Evolving Curricula with Regret-Based Environment Design) is like POET in that it evolves levels, but trains only a single agent (not a population), using a minimax-regret objective and an evolutionary "mutation" of already-high-regret levels to generate progressively more informative challenges; if it reaches a Nash equilibrium, the student follows a minimax-regret strategy. — [ACCEL project page](https://accelagent.github.io/), [Evolving Curricula with Regret-Based Environment Design (PMLR v162)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/parker-holder22a/parker-holder22a.pdf)
- A 2025 study on goal misgeneralization found "the most flexible adversary (ACCEL) paired with the most powerful regret estimator (oracle-latest) is remarkably robust to goal misgeneralization in all environments" tested — direct (if narrow) quantitative evidence of ACCEL's generalization benefit over simpler baselines. — [Mitigating Goal Misgeneralization via Minimax Regret (arXiv:2507.03068)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.03068v2)
- The original PAIRED/UED line has known instability problems: a 2023 paper is titled and framed specifically around "Stabilizing Unsupervised Environment Design with a Learned Adversary," implying the base approach has practical training-stability issues that later work has had to address. — [Stabilizing UED with a Learned Adversary (arXiv:2308.10797)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10797)
- Facebook Research maintains an open-source reference implementation of the whole family ("dcd" — Dual Curriculum Design), indicating the algorithms are implementable by external teams without reinventing core infrastructure, though the repo covers a nontrivial multi-agent training loop (teacher + protagonist + antagonist/multiple students), not a drop-in wrapper around a single-agent training script. — [facebookresearch/dcd on GitHub](https://github.com/facebookresearch/dcd)
- 2025–2026 follow-on work continues to target core weaknesses: "PACE: Parameter Change for Unsupervised Environment Design" and "Improving Environment Novelty Quantification for Effective Unsupervised Environment Design" both indicate the regret-estimation / novelty-quantification machinery is still an active research problem, not a solved, off-the-shelf component. — [PACE (arXiv:2605.01358)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01358v1), [Improving Environment Novelty Quantification (arXiv:2502.05726)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.05726)

### Inferences
- Relative to plain domain randomization (the team's current plan), UED methods (PAIRED/ACCEL-family) are a strictly higher-complexity option: they require (a) a second trainable agent (adversary/generator) or an evolutionary level-mutation loop, (b) a regret estimator (which itself requires either a second "antagonist" agent trained in parallel, or an oracle/approximate-oracle return estimate for each generated level), and (c) careful stabilization given documented training instability in the base algorithms. For a small team building a 2D racing simulator, this is a substantial additional engineering investment — plausibly weeks, not days — compared to parameterized domain randomization over track width/friction/curvature ranges.
- The generalization benefit is real and repeatedly claimed in the adjacent literature (maze navigation, grid-world environments), but the specific reported wins (e.g., ACCEL's robustness to goal misgeneralization) are from small discrete/grid environments, not continuous vehicle dynamics; porting a level-mutation "evolutionary" generator to a continuous track-geometry parameterization (e.g., mutating spline control points or curvature profiles) is conceptually plausible but not something found directly evidenced in the retrieved sources.
- A reasonable low-cost middle ground the team could consider (not directly evidenced, but implied by the design space): a *simplified* regret-proxy curriculum — e.g., track the RL agent's per-track-instance regret/return during plain domain-randomized training and oversample instances where the agent currently performs worst (a "prioritized replay over track instances" heuristic) — captures some of the curriculum-shaping spirit of UED without implementing a full adversarial generator or evolutionary mutation loop.

### Gaps
- Could not find a study directly comparing PAIRED/ACCEL against plain domain randomization on a continuous-control driving/vehicle task; all strong evidence found is from grid-world/maze/discrete navigation domains.
- Could not obtain precise implementation-effort estimates (engineer-hours, lines of code, wall-clock training cost) for PAIRED/ACCEL vs. DR from any primary source — the "much more complex" characterization above is an inference from the described algorithmic structure (multi-agent + regret estimation + ongoing stabilization research), not a directly cited effort comparison.

## What benchmarking/evaluation practices does this literature recommend for measuring "generalization gap" rigorously (seeds, statistical tests, held-out set design)?

### Takeaway
The clearest, most actionable recommendation set comes from Agarwal et al.'s "Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice" (NeurIPS 2021 Outstanding Paper) and its `rliable` library: report interval estimates (not just point estimates) of aggregate performance across seeds, use robust aggregate metrics like the interquartile mean, and use performance profiles rather than a single mean/median — directly applicable to the team's own evaluation plan when comparing policies across held-out track sets. Complementing this, the Cobbe/Procgen-style methodology of explicitly separating train-level and test-level (held-out) distributions, and reporting default/interpolation/extrapolation performance tiers, gives a concrete template for designing the held-out track set itself.

### Cited Findings
- Agarwal et al. argue that "most published results on deep RL benchmarks compare point estimates of aggregate performance... while ignoring statistical uncertainty," and that "the shift toward computationally-demanding benchmarks has exacerbated this by evaluating only a small number of runs per task"; reliable evaluation "cannot ignore uncertainty without risking progress." They demonstrate on Atari-100k that conclusions drawn from point estimates alone can diverge from those drawn from rigorous statistical analysis. — [Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice (arXiv:2108.13264)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)
- Their concrete recommendations: report interval estimates of aggregate performance, use "performance profiles" to visualize result variability across runs/tasks, and use more robust aggregate metrics such as the interquartile mean (IQM) instead of plain mean, since IQM is less sensitive to outlier runs than the mean and less noisy than the median with few runs. The `rliable` library operationalizes this and is explicitly designed to work "even with only a handful of seeds." — [rliable GitHub](https://github.com/google-research/rliable), [rliable project page](https://agarwl.github.io/rliable/)
- A follow-on 2023 paper, "AdaStop: adaptive statistical testing for sound comparisons of Deep RL agents," proposes adaptive sequential statistical testing specifically to determine when enough seeds/runs have been collected to make a sound comparison claim — directly relevant to a small team with limited compute deciding how many seeds is "enough." — [AdaStop (arXiv:2306.10882)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10882)
- Standard practice observed across the literature: train with multiple (commonly 3–5, sometimes more) distinct network-initialization/environment seeds and report mean and standard deviation across them; some offline-RL work uses as few as 3 seeds combined with explicit power analysis to justify significance claims via pairwise t-tests. — [search synthesis of generalization-gap evaluation literature](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.05742v2)
- Cobbe et al.'s Procgen/CoinRun methodology explicitly separates a bounded training level set from a much larger (often effectively unbounded/procedurally infinite) test level set, and measures the resulting train-test performance gap directly as the operational definition of "generalization gap" — a concrete, reusable template: fix a finite training distribution of instances (tracks), hold out a separate, non-overlapping distribution (or an extrapolated/harder distribution) for evaluation, and report performance on both. — [Quantifying Generalization in RL](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02341v3)
- A related evaluation framework decomposes reported metrics into three tiers: "Default" (in-distribution/default settings), "Interpolation" (randomized settings within the training range), and "Extrapolation" (geometric mean of success across configurations that go beyond the training range) — giving graded evidence of how far generalization extends, rather than a single binary in/out-of-distribution number. — [search synthesis, generalization gap evaluation protocol literature](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/376821499_Metrics_for_Assessing_Generalization_of_Deep_Reinforcement_Learning_in_Parameterized_Environments)

### Inferences
- For the racing team's own evaluation plan, the directly actionable adoptions are: (1) define a held-out set of track geometries that is disjoint from the domain-randomization training distribution, and additionally define an "extrapolation" tier (track parameter ranges/geometric features that go *beyond* what was ever seen in training, e.g., tighter curvature or novel topology types) — not just an interpolation tier within the same randomization ranges; (2) run enough seeds (the literature commonly uses 3–5 minimum, more if compute allows) and report interval estimates (e.g., via bootstrap confidence intervals, as `rliable` provides) rather than a single mean lap-time or success-rate number; (3) if comparing two agent variants (e.g., DR-only vs. DR+RMA-adaptation), use a statistical test (or the adaptive-sampling approach from AdaStop) to determine whether an observed difference is likely real given the seed budget, rather than eyeballing bar charts.
- The Default/Interpolation/Extrapolation tiering is directly reusable almost as-is for track generalization: "Default" = a small canonical set of hand-designed tracks, "Interpolation" = randomized tracks within the trained parameter ranges, "Extrapolation" = randomized tracks with parameters or geometric structures outside the trained ranges (sharper corners, longer straights, different track topologies/chicane patterns never seen in training).

### Gaps
- Could not find evaluation-protocol literature specifically tailored to *procedural track/geometry* generalization (as opposed to parameterized physics/terrain generalization in general robotics benchmarks) — the tiering and statistical-testing recommendations above are general RL-evaluation best practice, not racing- or track-generalization-specific, and their direct applicability to geometric (as opposed to scalar-parameter) held-out sets is an inference, not a verified finding.
